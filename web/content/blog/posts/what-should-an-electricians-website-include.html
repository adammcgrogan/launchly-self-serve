Most electrician websites make the same mistake: they read like a business card instead of proof that you're good at the job. If someone's found you through a search, they're usually comparing you against two or three other electricians in the same few minutes. What's on the page in that moment is what decides whether they call.

Photos of finished work, not stock images

A consumer unit you rewired, a clean run of conduit, a finished rewire before the plaster went back up — these do more than any paragraph about your experience. People can't judge electrical work by eye, but they can judge whether it looks careful and tidy, and they'll assume the wiring they can't see follows the same standard as the parts they can.

What you actually do, spelled out

"Electrical services" tells a visitor nothing. Rewires, consumer unit upgrades, EV charger installs, EICRs, fault finding — list the specific jobs you take on. Someone searching for "EICR near me" needs to see that word on your site, not infer it from "electrical services."

Where you actually work

Vague coverage ("serving the local area") reads as unsure. Name the towns or postcodes you actually cover. It helps a visitor self-select before they call, and it's also what tends to matter for showing up in local search results in the first place.

A way to reach you that isn't just a phone number

Some people don't want to call — they want to describe the job in writing, maybe with a photo, and hear back when it suits them. A contact form isn't a replacement for your phone number, it's for the customer who'd rather not ring a stranger mid-job.

Certifications, plainly stated

NICEIC, NAPIT, whichever body you're registered with — say it clearly, near the top, not buried in an about page. It's one of the few things a customer actually knows to check for, and it answers the "can I trust this person in my house" question before they've even called.
